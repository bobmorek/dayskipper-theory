Plate I

The chart: scales & datums

Almost every avoidable chartwork error is a datum error or a scale error. Three different vertical references are printed on the same sheet, and only one of the two edge scales measures distance.

The three vertical datums

Depths — below Chart Datum

Chart Datum is approximately Lowest Astronomical Tide (LAT): the lowest the tide is predicted to fall. Soundings are a worst case, so there is almost always more water than printed.

Heights — above MHWS

Lighthouses, cliffs, land features and the elevation used for a light's range are given above Mean High Water Springs.

Clearances — above HAT

Vertical clearance under a bridge or power cable is given above Highest Astronomical Tide, so the real gap is usually a little more than charted.

Three datums, one sheetDepths down from LAT, heights up from MHWS, overhead clearances up from HAT. Examiners test the mismatch: if a question gives you a mast height and a charted cable clearance, you need the height of tide and the HAT, not just the charted figure.

Measuring on the chart

Distance — the side scale only

1 minute of latitude = 1 nautical mile, so distance comes off the left or right edge, opened level with the area you are working in. On a Mercator chart the latitude scale stretches towards the poles, which is exactly why you must measure alongside your position and not at the top of the sheet.

Never use the longitude scale along the top or bottom for distance. A minute of longitude is only a mile at the equator.

Direction — the compass rose

Outer ring: True. Aligned to true north. Inner ring: Magnetic, offset by the local variation, which is printed at the centre with its annual change and the year it was correct.

All chartwork is plotted in True. Convert compass and magnetic bearings before the pencil touches the chart.

Position notation

Latitude first, then longitude, to a decimal of a minute: 50°45'.3N 001°17'.6W. Latitude N/S of the equator, longitude E/W of Greenwich.

Scale

1:25,000 is a larger scale than 1:50,000 — more detail, less area. Large scale, large detail. Use the largest-scale chart available for pilotage.

Corrections

Admiralty charts are kept current by weekly Notices to Mariners, applied in magenta ink and logged in the bottom-left corner. Check the source data diagram for survey quality.

Instruments

DividersDistance only — off the latitude scale.
Plotter / ruleDirection only — Portland or Breton plotter, or parallel rule walked to the compass rose.
2B pencilSoft, so it erases cleanly. A chart is a working surface, not a record.
AlmanacTidal predictions, secondary port differences, light details, harbour information.

Chart symbols you must know

The full set is Admiralty Chart 5011, which is a booklet rather than a chart. These are the ones that decide a Day Skipper answer; the drawings below are simplified, and 5011 is the authority on the exact glyph.

Depths and the seabed

53 Sounding 5.3 m below chart datum. Metres and decimetres.
23 Drying height Underlined: dries 2.3 m above chart datum. Covered only when the tide exceeds 2.3 m.
Depth contour Joins points of equal depth below datum. The 5 m and 10 m lines are your safety fences.
Blue tint Shoal water inside a chosen contour. Green tint = dries at low water.

Dangers

Underwater rock Cross with dots: dangerous rock at or near datum. Stay off it at any state of tide.
Drying rock Star symbol with an underlined height: covers and uncovers, here drying 1.5 m above datum.
Dangerous wreck Hull-and-masts symbol; with a depth figure if the clearance is surveyed. Dotted outline = dangerous.
Foul ground Obstructions, old moorings, debris. Safe to navigate over, never safe to anchor in.

Marks and areas

Anchorage A recommended or customary anchorage. Not a guarantee of holding or shelter.
Lit mark The magenta flare marks a lit buoy, beacon or light. Characters and colours are on the buoyage plate.
Leading line Two marks in transit on a printed bearing: the safest position line there is, because it needs no compass.
Magenta limits Magenta is the attention colour: restricted areas, cables, pipelines, traffic schemes, ferry routes.
The colour codeBuff/yellow is land, white is deep water, blue is shoal, green dries, magenta warns. If a line is magenta, read the label before you cross it.

Plate IV

Plotting symbols: reading a plot at a glance

Every line on a plot carries its own label. Count the arrowheads to know which line you are looking at, and read the shape to know how much the position is worth.

Positions

Fix Dot inside a circle. An observed position from two or more position lines. Always labelled with the time and the log reading.
DR A single cross-bar on the water track. Course steered and distance logged only: no tide, no leeway.
EP A triangle. The DR after tidal stream and leeway have been applied: your best estimate short of a fix.

Tracks and vectors — count the arrowheads

One arrow · water track The course steered through the water (after leeway). Length = STW × time.
Two arrows · ground track The track made good over the ground. Length = SOG × time. This is the line that either clears the rock or does not.
Three arrows · tidal stream Drawn in the direction the tide flows towards (the set), length = rate × time (the drift).
One water · Two ground · Three tideWorking up the count: through the water, over the ground, and the tide that turns one into the other.

Position lines

Position line A single arrowhead pointing at the object. You are somewhere along it; one alone is not a position.
Transferred PL Two arrowheads: the earlier line moved forward by the course and distance run. Used in a running fix.
Transit Two charted objects in line. A free, exact position line with no compass error in it — and the best check of your compass there is.

Fix quality

The cocked hat

Three bearings rarely meet at a point; the little triangle they form is the cocked hat. A small one means a good fix. A large one means an error somewhere — a misidentified mark, a stale bearing, or deviation you have not allowed for. Re-take it rather than average it.

If you must use a large cocked hat, take the corner nearest the danger. Pessimism is free.

Choosing the objects

Spread the objects roughly 60° apart (or 90° for two). Bearings that cut at a fine angle give a long, unreliable intersection.

Take the bearing that is changing slowest first — objects ahead and astern — and the fastest-changing beam object last, then note the time and the log. Positively identify every mark before you trust it.

Plate V

Compass: True, Magnetic, Compass

The chart is True. The boat is Compass. Two errors sit between them, and each has a sign.

Variation

The angle between true north and magnetic north. A property of where you are on the planet, printed at the centre of the compass rose with its annual change. It is the same for every compass on board.

Deviation

Error caused by the boat's own magnetic field — engine, keel, electronics, that tin of beans by the binnacle. It changes with the vessel's heading, so it is read from a deviation card for the heading being steered, never used as one fixed number. It applies to the steering compass; a hand-bearing compass used well away from steel is usually taken as deviation-free.

True → Virgins → Make → Dull → Company · Add WhiskyTrue, Variation, Magnetic, Deviation, Compass. Going down the list (True to Compass) you add West errors and subtract East. Going back up, the reverse.
Error West, compass Best · Error East, compass LeastWith a westerly error the compass reads a bigger (best) number than True; with an easterly error it reads a smaller one. So True 100° with 5°W variation is Magnetic 105°; Compass 090° with 6°E variation is True 096°.

Chart → boat

True → Magnetic → Compass. Add westerly variation and deviation. This is the direction you work a course to steer.

Boat → chart

Compass → Magnetic → True. Add easterly errors. This is the direction you work a bearing before plotting it.

Checking for error

Run a known transit and compare the compass reading with the charted bearing; or compare a hand-bearing compass on a distant object. The difference is your error on that heading.

Leeway is not a compass errorVariation and deviation change what the compass reads. Leeway changes where the boat actually goes: the wind pushes her bodily downwind, so her water track lies to leeway of her heading. Correct for it separately, and always into the wind when working a course to steer.
Plate VI

The jobs, step by step

Five exercises cover almost every chartwork question in the paper. Each is a fixed recipe; the marks are in following it in order and labelling as you go.

1 · Three-bearing fix

Where am I, right now, from things I can see?

  1. Pick three charted objects you can positively identify, spread roughly 60° apart.
  2. Take the slowest-changing bearing first (ahead or astern), the beam bearing last. Note the time and log.
  3. Correct each compass bearing to True (add easterly error, subtract westerly).
  4. Plot each as a position line from its object, single arrowhead, labelled with the time.
  5. Judge the cocked hat. Small: take its centre. Large: re-shoot, or take the corner nearest the danger.
  6. Mark the fix as a dot in a circle with the time and log alongside.
You getAn observed position. Everything else on the plot is measured from it.

2 · Estimated position (EP)

Where am I now, given what I have steered and what the tide has done?

  1. Start from the last fix. Note the time and log then, and now.
  2. Convert the course steered from compass to True, then offset it downwind by the leeway angle: that is the water track.
  3. Lay it off from the fix, one arrowhead, and mark off the distance run through the water from the log. That point is the DR (one cross-bar).
  4. From the almanac or tidal diamond take the set (direction of flow) and rate for the hour. Drift = rate × time.
  5. From the DR lay off the tidal stream vector, three arrowheads, length = drift.
  6. Its end is your EP: triangle, with the time. Join fix to EP for the ground track, two arrowheads.
You getPosition, plus your actual track and speed over the ground — which is what tells you whether you are being set onto anything.

The EP construction

Course steered and log distance give the DR; the tidal stream vector carries it to the EP. The line you actually travelled over the ground is the one from the fix straight to the triangle.

3 · Course to steer (CTS)

What heading gets me to that point, given the tide that is about to push me sideways?

  1. Draw the ground track you want: a line from your start position through the destination, extended well beyond it.
  2. From the start, lay off the tidal stream vector for the passage time — direction the tide sets, length = rate × time. Three arrowheads.
  3. Open the dividers to your speed through the water × the same time, put the point on the end of the tide vector, and swing an arc to cut the ground-track line.
  4. Join the end of the tide vector to that intersection. That line is your water track: read its direction off the rose. One arrowhead.
  5. Apply leeway: steer up into the wind by the leeway angle so the boat's water track is the line you just drew.
  6. Convert True → Magnetic → Compass (add westerly errors). That number is what you give the helm.
  7. Measure start to intersection along the ground track: that distance over the same time is your SOG, and the distance to the destination gives the ETA.
Watch the orderThe tide vector goes on first, from the start position. Laying it off the wrong end is the classic lost mark.

4 · Running fix

Only one mark in sight, and one bearing is not a position.

  1. Take a bearing on the object; correct to True; plot the position line. Note the time and log.
  2. Hold your course and run on until the bearing has changed by 30° or more — the bigger the change, the better the cut.
  3. Take the second bearing, correct and plot it. Note time and log.
  4. Work out how far and in which direction you have travelled over the ground in between (course steered plus tide and leeway, as for an EP).
  5. Transfer the first position line: redraw it parallel to itself, moved along by that ground track and distance. Two arrowheads.
  6. Where the transferred line cuts the second bearing is the running fix. Mark and label it, and treat it as less reliable than a proper fix.
You getA usable position from a single object — and a bad one if your tidal estimate for the run was wrong.

The course-to-steer construction

The arc is swung from the end of the tide vector, not from the start. Where it cuts the required ground track sets the water track, and the distance from the start to that cut over the same time is your speed over the ground.

5 · Pilotage: clearing bearings & transits

Not where am I, but am I still in the safe water — answered without a plot.

  1. Before you go in, find a charted object beyond the danger and draw a line from it that passes safely clear.
  2. Read the bearing of that line from the object, in True, then convert to the compass you will actually use.
  3. Label it NMT (not more than) or NLT (not less than) so the rule is unambiguous under way: e.g. "Keep the church bearing NLT 040°".
  4. Where two charted objects line up, use the transit instead: it needs no compass, has no error in it, and tells you instantly which side of the line you are on.
  5. Back it up with a depth contour as a second fence: the echo sounder is a position line too.
  6. Write the pilotage plan down as a strip of marks, bearings and distances before you enter. You will not have time to plot inside.
You getA go/no-go check you can read in a second, which is the only kind that works in a narrow entrance.
Plate VII

Tidal heights & the depth sums

Every depth question is one of four sums. Get the definitions right and the arithmetic is trivial.

Depth of water = charted depth + height of tide
Charted depth is below datum; height of tide is above it. They add.
Over a drying patch = height of tide − drying height
A negative answer means you are aground. Underlined figure = drying height.
Clearance under keel = depth of water − draught
Then take off a safety margin of your own choosing before you commit.
Overhead clearance = charted clearance + (HAT − height of tide)
Charted clearance is above HAT, so you normally have more room, not less.

Range, springs and neaps

Range

HW height minus LW height for that tide. Not the same thing as the height of tide, which is the height above chart datum at a given moment.

Springs

Biggest range, strongest streams. A day or two after new and full moon, when sun and moon pull in line.

Neaps

Smallest range, weakest streams. A day or two after the first and last quarter moons.

Around most of the UK the tide is semi-diurnal: roughly two highs and two lows a day, about 6 hours 12 minutes between HW and LW.

Rule of twelfths

An approximation for a normal 6-hour semi-diurnal tide: the range divided into twelfths, hour by hour.

2nd hr
2/12
3rd hr
3/12
4th hr
3/12
5th hr
2/12
6th hr
1/12

Half the range moves in the middle two hours; the first three hours together give 6/12, exactly half. Tidal streams follow the same shape, which is why the stream runs hardest around mid-tide.

Where it failsTwelfths assumes a sinusoidal 6-hour tide. It is wrong where the curve is distorted — the Solent's double high waters, or anywhere the almanac prints a special curve. For a real answer use the tidal curve.

Using a tidal curve

  1. Take the HW and LW times and heights for the port and date from the almanac.
  2. Mark the HW height on the top scale and the LW height on the bottom, and join them with a sloping line.
  3. Fill in the hour boxes along the bottom, working out from HW.
  4. Choose the spring (solid) or neap (dashed) curve, interpolating between them by comparing today's range with the printed spring and neap ranges.
  5. Given a time, go up to the curve, across to the sloping line, and down to read the height. Given a height, run the same route backwards to read the time.
Use it for"Can I cross the bar at 1430?" and "When is there 3.5 m over the sill?"

Secondary ports

  1. Find the standard port the almanac lists your harbour against.
  2. Look up the standard port's HW and LW times and heights for the day.
  3. Apply the secondary port's time differences, interpolating for the actual time of HW or LW.
  4. Apply the height differences, interpolating between the springs and neaps columns for today's range.
  5. Work the resulting times and heights on the standard port's curve.
Watch forDifferences are sometimes given as ratios or factors, and time differences can be negative. Copy the sign.

Streams, not heights

Tidal diamond

A lettered magenta diamond on the chart; the table gives set (direction) and rate for each hour before and after HW at a named standard port, in springs and neaps columns.

Tidal stream atlas

Twelve pages, one per hour relative to HW. Arrows give direction; figures give neap and spring rates in tenths of a knot (e.g. 19,34 = 1.9 kn neaps, 3.4 kn springs).

Flood and ebb

Flood is the incoming stream, ebb the outgoing. Slack water is around the turn, not necessarily at HW. Streams run hardest at springs and around mid-tide.

Plate VIII

The numbers

D = S × T  ·  T = D / S  ·  S = D / T
In minutes: time = 60 × distance / speed. 9 NM logged in 1 h 12 m (1.2 h) = 7.5 kn.
1° latitude = 60 NM  ·  1' latitude = 1 NM
Which is why distance comes off the side scale, level with your position.

Nautical mile

1852 m, about 1.15 statute miles. One knot = one nautical mile per hour.

Cable

One tenth of a nautical mile: 185.2 m, near enough 200 yards. Pilotage distances are given in cables.

Fathom

1.83 m (6 feet). Only on older charts — modern Admiralty soundings are metres and decimetres.

The 1-in-60 rule

1° of course error puts you 1 mile off track after 60 miles — so roughly 1 cable off per mile run, per 6° of error. Useful in reverse too: if you are 2 miles off after 20 miles, you were about 6° out.

Dip, and the two ranges of a light

Nominal range in the almanac assumes 10 M visibility. Geographic (rising and dipping) range is pure geometry from the height of the light and your height of eye. From a yacht, geometry usually wins. Timing the moment a light rises or dips gives you a distance-off position line.

Plate IX

Terms & last-hour recall

HeadingThe direction the bow points. Not necessarily where the boat is going.
Water trackThe direction the boat actually moves through the water: heading offset by leeway. One arrowhead.
Ground trackThe direction she moves over the ground: water track plus tidal stream. Two arrowheads. This is the one that hits the rock.
SetThe direction a tidal stream flows towards, in degrees True. (Wind is named for where it comes from; tide for where it goes.)
RateThe speed of the tidal stream in knots. Drift is the distance it carries you over a given time: rate × time.
LeewayThe angle between the heading and the water track, caused by the wind pushing the boat bodily to leeward. Always allowed for into the wind when working a course to steer.
STW / SOGSpeed through the water (the log) versus speed over the ground (GPS). The difference is the tide.
COG / CMGCourse over the ground, instantaneously; course made good, averaged between two known positions.
DRDead reckoning: course steered and distance run only. No tide, no leeway.
EPEstimated position: the DR with tidal stream and leeway applied. Marked with a triangle.
FixA position from two or more position lines. Dot in a circle, with the time.
Position lineA line somewhere on which you must lie: a bearing, a transit, a depth contour, a range from a light.
TransitTwo charted objects seen in line. An exact position line with no compass error in it.
Cocked hatThe triangle left by three bearings that do not meet. Small is good; large means something is wrong.
Running fixA position from one object, using a bearing transferred forward by the course and distance run.
Charted depth / soundingDepth below chart datum. Drying height: height above chart datum, printed underlined.
Height of tideThe height of the water above chart datum at a given moment. Range is the difference between successive HW and LW heights.
Standard / secondary portPorts with full printed predictions, and ports worked from them using time and height differences.
Tidal diamondA lettered magenta diamond keyed to a stream table of set and rate for each hour relative to HW.
Variation / deviationError between true and magnetic north (place) and between magnetic and compass north (the boat, and its heading).
Rhumb lineA line of constant bearing. It is a straight line on a Mercator chart, which is what makes the projection useful for plotting.
Waypoint / XTEA position stored in the GPS, and the cross-track error: how far you are off the direct line between waypoints.
DipThe moment a light rises above or dips below the horizon, giving a distance off from the geographic range tables.

If you only revise eight things

  1. Distance off the latitude scale, level with your position. Never longitude.
  2. Depths below chart datum (LAT); heights above MHWS; overhead clearances above HAT.
  3. Depth of water = charted depth + height of tide. Over a drying patch, height of tide − drying height.
  4. Fix = dot in a circle. DR = one bar. EP = triangle. Label everything with the time.
  5. One arrowhead water track, two ground track, three tide.
  6. Course to steer: ground track first, then the tide vector from the start, then swing the boat-speed arc onto the ground track.
  7. True → Compass: add West. Compass → True: add East. Deviation is read for the heading being steered.
  8. Rule of twelfths 1-2-3-3-2-1, and its limits; the tidal curve for anything that actually matters.
  9. Leeway is applied into the wind when working a course to steer, and downwind of the heading when plotting where you have been.
