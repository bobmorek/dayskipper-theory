Passage PlanOne side of paper, filled before you slip

The standard four-column plan sheet, in the order a skipper actually works it: how far and how long, then the weather and the tide that decide whether you go at all, then the stores, the notices, the hazards, the comms and the way out if it goes wrong. Side two carries the departure and arrival pilotage briefs, each with its picture. Type into it, print it, or print it blank and write on it in pencil. The chartwork behind the boxes is on the chartwork plates.

Plate II

Tidal clock

Stream tables and tidal diamonds are not written in clock time; they are written in hours before and after high water. This converts between the two. Set the standard port's HW, add the secondary port's time difference, then slide the clock — or slide the hours-from-HW instead and read off what time that is. Departure and arrival get their own markers, so you can see the whole passage sitting on the curve and move it until the tide is where you want it.

It knows nothing about real tides. It is arithmetic on the numbers you type in, so every figure comes off the almanac first, and all four want to be in the same time zone — the one you wrote in the sheet's Times box.

2045

Straight off the almanac's own page for the standard port.

+0h10

The time difference for your port. Positive is later than the standard port.

1600

The moment you are planning for — an ETD, an ETA, or the hour you will be off a headland.

1600

When you slip. Its marker sits on the curve with everything else.

2200

When you expect to be there. The shaded band between the two is the passage.

−4h55

The row you read in the stream table. Slide it to find the clock time of a tidal gate.

The stream row is the hour centred on that number: “HW−5” covers 5h30 to 4h30 before high water. The twelfths line assumes a six-hour, roughly sinusoidal tide — fine for a mental check, not for deciding whether you float. Low waters are shown at a nominal 6h12 either side of HW; the almanac's are the real ones.

Plate III

What goes in each box

The sheet is the planning half of APEM written down. Appraisal is the thinking you do before you touch it (is this passage sensible for this boat, this crew, this forecast?); execution and monitoring happen afterwards, at the chart table, by checking the real position against what this sheet predicted.

Time = Distance ÷ Speed
Plan on a conservative speed — the one you can hold in the forecast wind, not the one on the sales brochure. Then add a margin and check the arrival still works with the tidal gate.

Speed, time, distance

Measure the distance along the legs you will actually sail, off the latitude scale. Divide by planning speed for passage time, add it to ETD for ETA. If the ETA lands outside the lock, bar or bridge window, the ETD is wrong, not the tide.

Boat, MMSI and callsign go here because a Mayday or a Traffic report needs them read straight off the sheet, not remembered.

Crew

Names, and next to them what each person can do: who can helm, who can navigate, who gets seasick, who holds what qualification, and any medical need. A watch roster only works once you know that.

Weather

Record the forecast you used, its source and issue time, for departure, enroute and arrival. Wind direction and force, sea state, visibility, and what the fronts are doing. Note the wind against tide stretches: a F5 over a 2 kn spring ebb is a different sea from the same F5 an hour later.

Shipping forecast, inshore waters, MSI broadcast on VHF, and the harbour's own actuals all belong here — dated, so you know when it goes stale.

Tide & tidal streams

For each end of the passage, and any port in between: HW and LW times and heights, so you can work depth of water and whether the passage is a springs or neaps one. Then the streams, hour by hour relative to HW, as set and rate.

Tidal gates get planned backwards — pick the time you must be at the gate, then work back to the ETD.

Onboard

A checklist, not a wish list: fuel and range, water, oils, spares, tools, charts and almanac corrected, log, food, safety equipment served and in date, personal kit and protection (lifejackets, harnesses, sun, warmth), the watch roster, the VHF and DSC test, the SOLAS V passage-planning requirement, and the ship's papers.

SOLAS V Regulation 34 is why the sheet exists at all: a leisure skipper must plan a passage taking account of weather, tides, limitations of the vessel, crew, navigational dangers and a contingency plan.

Notices to mariners

Local notices at both ends, plus anything enroute: firing and exercise areas, dredging, works, cable laying, buoys moved or lit differently, races and regattas. Note the check, and where you checked it.

Hazards & threats

Overfalls, races and tide rips, off-lying rocks and banks, shipping lanes and separation schemes, ferry tracks, fishing gear, lee shores, bar entrances, and the ones people forget: night arrival, crew fatigue, a flat battery, fog.

Comms plan

Every station you might call, in order: harbour or marina, VTS, coastguard, and the working channels for each. Add opening hours, phone numbers, and when you will report in to anyone ashore.

Key waypoints & route

Each leg as: from, to, true track, distance, and the visual cue that confirms it — a transit, a clearing bearing, a light's sector, a depth contour. Waypoints go off the danger and never on the buoy itself.

Nightfall

Write sunset, the end of civil twilight and the end of nautical twilight next to the ETA, with the moon's age and rise or set time. Civil twilight is when you lose the horizon and the unlit marks; nautical twilight is when it is properly dark.

Then plan for it: the light characteristic of every mark you will use, red light at the chart table, torches and lifejacket lights found before it goes dark, and the harbour's own hours. A leg that is easy in daylight is a different leg at 2200.

Almanac and web tables give these in UT too. In British summer time, add the hour here as well — a sunset an hour out is the difference between a dusk arrival and a night one.

Contingency planning

Bolt-holes and the point of no return for each: the weather refuge that is still accessible in the forecast wind and the state of tide you would arrive at. Then injury and MOB, engine or rig failure, loss of electronics, and the abort decision — where you turn back, and by what time.

APEM — Appraisal · Planning · Execution · Monitoring Appraise the whole passage before you plan any of it; plan it on paper; execute; then monitor by comparing the real position against this sheet, and change the plan when they disagree.
The examiner's favourite A plan that arrives at the destination at LW springs, at night, with the last bolt-hole 15 miles astern. The times, the tide and the contingency are marked together; get them agreeing with each other, not just filled in.

Seven things to settle before this one sails

The plan is transcribed as written. These are the places where the sheet argues with itself, in the order they would bite. None of them is settled here: each is a check against the chart, the almanac and the forecast.

  1. The two outbound longitudes. The sheet reads 005°40′W and 005°47′W. Those plot tens of miles west of any Trewick–Roger Point track; 004°40′W and 004°47′W fit the 210° and 220° legs and their 8 and 9 mile runs. The arrival waypoint, 005°01′W, is right for off Trellan Head. Plot all three before you load them into anything.
  2. Six hours for a four-and-a-half hour passage. 1600 to 2200 at 5 knots is 30 miles; round Roger Point it is about 22. Either the ETA is carrying an hour and a half of slack, or the speed is optimistic, and the empty Distance and Time boxes are exactly the arithmetic that would say which.
  3. HW an hour apart at ports ten minutes apart. The sheet has HW Calmouth 2046 and HW Trewick 2100, and the footnote has HW Stannock 1945. All three are within about ten minutes of each other in reality, so 1945 against 2045 looks like UT against BST: HW Stannock 1945 UT + 1 h = 2045 BST, which is the Calmouth figure you already wrote. Almanac predictions are UT all year; between late March and late October you add the hour yourself, to every HW, LW and stream hour on the sheet. Mix the two and the plan's whole tidal argument moves by an hour — which is why the sheet now carries a Times box: fill it in once, in capitals, and convert everything into it.
  4. A fair stream, west-bound, on the flood. Three knots leaving Trewick and a knot fair all the way to Calmouth is the plan's whole margin. Check it on the tidal diamonds for the hours 1600–2200, at the corrected HW: if any of the middle legs is foul, the ETA goes.
  5. Nightfall eats the second half of this passage. For 15 September 2026 at this latitude, sunset is about 1857, civil twilight ends about 1929, and it is properly dark by about 2008 (computed, BST — check the almanac, whose tables are UT). The moon is four days old and 17% lit, and it follows the sun down, so there is no useful moonlight. So the plan is: daylight to Roger Point, dusk across Verity Bay, and the whole Calmouth entrance in the dark — past George, with commercial traffic moving. Two consequences: every mark in the arrival brief needs its light characteristic written next to it (Trellan Head, George, the Pardinal; the special mark may be unlit), and the hour and a half of slack in the ETA is not spare time — spend it by leaving earlier, not by arriving later. Leave at 1600 and lose an hour anywhere and you are entering at 2300 on a falling tide.
  6. Contingency is empty. Weather refuge, injury, gear failure, abort point — the box the whole sheet exists for is blank, on a passage that ends in the dark with an unreliable light behind it. Menverrow and Charlton Quay are the obvious bolt-holes short of Roger Point; what matters is the time by which you must choose one.
  7. The rest of the blanks. MMSI and callsign are what a Mayday needs read straight off the sheet, and nothing in the onboard column is ticked. Tick it, or admit it has not been checked.
What the sketches are and are not The two entrance pictures on side two are schematic: the shape of the pilotage and the marks named in the brief, drawn from general knowledge of those harbours, with the tracks laid over them. They are not to scale, they carry no depths, and they are not for navigation. Check every mark against the current chart and the almanac before you use any of it.
Plate V

If you only revise nine things

  1. SOLAS V makes a passage plan a legal requirement for a leisure yacht going to sea: weather, tides, limitations of the vessel, crew, navigational dangers, contingency plan.
  2. APEM: appraisal, planning, execution, monitoring. The sheet is the planning; the monitoring is what keeps you alive.
  3. Time = distance ÷ speed, at a speed you can really hold, then add a margin.
  4. Plan backwards from the tidal gate, not forwards from breakfast.
  5. Every forecast entry carries its source and issue time, or it is worthless by lunchtime.
  6. Streams are set and rate, tabulated by the hour relative to HW at the reference port.
  7. Waypoints sit clear of the danger, and each leg carries a visual check — transit, clearing bearing or light sector.
  8. A bolt-hole you cannot enter in the forecast wind, at the tide you would arrive on, is not a bolt-hole.
  9. Sunset and the end of twilight go on the sheet next to the ETA. Half a passage plan is a lighting plan.
Study aid only. This sheet follows the common four-column club/school passage plan layout and the SOLAS V Regulation 34 headings; schools vary the boxes, so fill it the way your instructor marks it.
